Industrial control security, computer-vision forensics, embedded tooling. Scroll down and each system builds itself in front of you — then hands you the controls and tells you, plainly, where it stops.

Begin the descent
Station 01 — industrial control

A relay that opens before the physics can finish.

A conventional intrusion detector watches traffic and raises an alert. By the time anyone reads it, the command has already reached the actuator. Aegis sits inline instead, and refuses.

The ordinary day

Two variables, coupled

Temperature and pressure are not independent. The vessel tolerates a lot of either one alone, and far less of both together. That relationship is a curve, not a pair of numbers.

Every threshold alarm in a traditional plant watches one variable at a time. Each of them can be perfectly satisfied while the process walks straight off the edge.

The command

Nothing here looks like an attack

A supervisory setpoint arrives on the fieldbus. It is well formed. It is signed, if anyone bothered to check. It raises temperature by a modest amount.

Taken alone it is unremarkable — which is precisely the point. Coordinated multi-variable stress is the Stuxnet shape: each step defensible, the sequence catastrophic.

The boundary

The envelope is the alarm

Aegis holds a model of the stability manifold and tests the commanded state against it, not the current one. The moment the operating point crosses that curve, the setpoint is refused.

Telemetry older than 120 s fails the same test. No fresh sensor data, no cross-variable check, no command. Fail closed, never open.

The cut

12.74 milliseconds

Refusal is not enough on its own, so the gateway de-energises an optocoupler relay on GPIO 25 and physically removes the device from the control network. Measured on the bench at 12.74 ms.

Only then does a Random Forest classify what it just saw — stuxnet, fdi_spike, thermal_drift — and hand the label to the report. The model names the attack. It never decides the response.

Station 02 — computer vision

Learning only what normal looks like.

Anomalies are, by definition, the thing you have fewest examples of. So Sentinel never learns them. It learns ordinary movement until it can reproduce it, and treats failure to reproduce as the signal.

Pixels

A camera sees no people

It sees intensity. Everything else — that there are four humans, that one of them is behaving oddly — is inference stacked on inference, and every layer inherits the mistakes of the one below.

Detection

Boxes, at 25 frames a second

YOLOv8n draws a box around each person. Fast enough to keep up with the camera on modest hardware, which matters more than a point of accuracy — a detector that falls behind real time stops being a detector.

Identity

A box is not a trajectory

DeepSORT carries identity across frames. Only then does motion mean anything: loitering, sprinting, a scuffle all live in how a person moves over time, not in any single frame.

Nine numbers per track per frame — normalised centroid, box dimensions, velocity, speed, acceleration, aspect ratio. Aspect ratio is the quiet one: it separates a person standing from a person on the ground.

Surprise

The model fails, usefully

An LSTM autoencoder reconstructs each sequence. Ordinary walking reconstructs almost perfectly. A sprint does not, and the reconstruction error spikes.

Rules run alongside: the model catches the unknown, the rules keep the named classes stable. Turn fusion off in the console and a harmless blip fires an alarm. That is the trade, made visible.

Where it stops

Unsupervised detection trades a labelling problem for a threshold problem, and reconstruction error has no natural cut-off. Accuracy also inherits YOLOv8's: under heavy occlusion the detector misses people, and anything it misses the tracker never sees. Errors compound down the pipeline rather than cancelling out.

Station 03 — image forensics

The edit is invisible. Its history is not.

A forged invoice is rarely a crude cut-and-paste. It is a competent edit, exported once, and it looks right to a reviewer with forty more to clear before lunch. What gives it away is not the picture — it is how many times each part of the picture has been compressed.

The document

One save, one history

A scanned invoice is a JPEG. Every block in it has been through the same quantisation, the same number of times. That uniformity is the baseline — and it is a property of the file, not of what the file depicts.

The edit

Fifty-one thousand becomes ninety-one

Someone opens it, paints over the total, types a new number, saves. The new pixels are sharp and have never been compressed. Everything around them has been, twice.

To the eye the document is seamless. Numerically, one rectangle now has a different past from the page it sits on.

The method

Compress it again and subtract

Error level analysis re-saves the image at a known quality and differences the result against the original. Regions already at that quantisation barely move. Fresh, un-compressed regions lose far more — and light up.

The caveat

Which is why one stream is not enough

ELA reacts to any recompression, innocent ones included. Alone it flags half the internet. So the classifier runs two ResNet18 streams — raw pixels and ELA — and fuses them, weighing forensic residue against what the content itself looks like.

Drag the quality slider down in the console and watch the whole page light up. That is the false-positive mode, not a bug in the demo.

Station 04 — risk quantification

Translating an attack into currency.

Security tooling speaks in severities and CVEs. The credit committee speaks in money. Nothing bridges the two while the incident is still running — which is when the bridge would actually be worth something.

The stream

Five agents, one contract each

Telemetry arrives over MQTT. A LangGraph DAG moves it through detector, classifier, isolator, quantifier, reporter — each a separate stage with an explicit contract with the next.

Staged as a graph rather than one procedure, any stage can be swapped, re-run or inspected alone, and the state at every hop is a readable object instead of a local variable buried in a call stack.

The decision

Isolation is an action, not a ticket

Above an anomaly score of 0.65 the isolator publishes micro-segmentation commands and cuts the device off. Containment that waits for a human has already spent the time that mattered.

The arithmetic

Downtime, penalties, regulator

The quantifier multiplies estimated downtime by an hourly loss rate, applies the SLA penalty as a share of it, and adds the regulatory band for the regime the asset sits under.

None of it is predicted. All of it is arithmetic on parameters a human supplied, which is the honest description of every financial risk model that has ever shipped.

The discipline

Four lines, not one number

The components are reported separately because they carry very different confidence. Downtime cost is an estimate of an estimate. A regulatory band is close to a known quantity.

Rolled into one headline figure, a reader cannot discount them independently — and a single confident number is exactly how this class of tool gets believed when it shouldn't be.

Where it stops

The financial model is only as good as its per-device cost parameters, which are entered rather than learned. The engine does the arithmetic faithfully and has no way to know when the inputs are optimistic. Treating its output as a forecast rather than a parameterised estimate is the failure mode this tool invites.

Station 05 — navigation

Routing that assumes the network is gone.

Consumer navigation makes two assumptions that fail at exactly the moment navigation matters: that you have connectivity, and that you don't mind a third party knowing where you are going.

The tiles

A map that already lives here

Valhalla runs in a container on the device with road tiles for the region pre-loaded. No request leaves. No account is attached to the journey. The routing graph is simply present.

The route

Two paths, drawn together

Shortest and main-highway are rendered simultaneously rather than one replacing the other. Shortest is not always preferable, and showing both lets an operator make a judgement the router cannot make for them.

The edge

Leaving the tiles

Ask for somewhere outside the loaded bounds and the local engine simply fails. The interesting engineering is in the seam: the backend intercepts that error and re-sends the waypoints to OSRM's global service.

You still get a route. What changed is where the computation happened — and whether anything about your destination left the device.

The sweep

Along the path, not around a pin

Standard tooling does point-radius scans. A radius around a destination says nothing about the twelve kilometres you have to traverse to reach it. Telemetry is extracted along the computed route, which is a different question with a different answer.

Where it stops

Offline routing is only as current as the tiles. A road closed last month is open as far as the local engine is concerned, and the fallback cannot help because the request never looked out of bounds. Tile freshness is a human process, not a software one. Wireless telemetry collection is also governed by local law, and what may be observed passively in one jurisdiction may not be in another.
